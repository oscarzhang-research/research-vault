听巴菲特1998年演讲后的感悟

Reflections After Listening to Buffett's 1998 Speech
Written 2026-08-23 and published unedited. Translated from the Chinese original — see the 中 link above.

What he said

  1. If you could only go long one person you know, and only short one person you know, who would you pick (write down the reasons for both the long and the short)? What we find in the end is that these factors are mostly about character and quality, not IQ, looks, or athletic ability. And the qualities or behaviors of the person you'd go long — generosity, honesty, kindness — can be cultivated, while the qualities or behaviors of the person you'd short can be avoided. Start building good habits and breaking bad ones early, or you'll regret it later (bad habits have inertia).
  2. Buffett's view on Japanese companies was that he wouldn't invest in them (at that time), because their ROE was generally too low. (He also stressed that he doesn't study macro.)
  3. Time is the friend of wonderful business, it's the enemy of the lousy business. Even if you buy a bad business at a low price, the end result will still be bad; even if you buy a good business at a somewhat higher price, the end result won't be bad either. (In investing, cheap things are rarely good, and expensive things are often expensive for a reason.)
  4. Don't risk what you have and what you need to make money you don't have and don't need. (Say a revolver has ten thousand chambers, and putting one bullet in and pulling the trigger on yourself gets you $1 million — a lot of people might actually do it. In reality, this is something you should never do, even at the cost of your life.)
  5. Following on from that: math and investing are two different things. Investing especially shouldn't rely too heavily on math.
  6. Do what you love, not what makes your résumé look better. Find something you're passionate about that has nothing to do with money, and keep pursuing it. Because once you stop being content, once you start believing that more money means more happiness, it becomes very easy to make mistakes — chasing quick wins, cutting corners.
  7. Pick companies you actually understand to invest in. (Stick with the business you understand.)
  8. A simple business is not the same as an easy business. Coca-Cola is a simple business but not an easy one — anyone can understand it, but its moat keeps widening. Businesses like this are also easier to project ten years or more into the future; they don't go through drastic change, and instead are more stable and durable.
  9. Buying stock is buying a company.
  10. Figuring out how much to pay for a company is extremely hard. Companies with very high certainty are, more often than not, not cheap. But Buffett said the first goal of investing is not to lose money — which means leaning toward companies with higher certainty.
  11. Pricing power is a very direct expression of a moat (for consumer companies, mindshare and emotional value are also expressions of a moat), though there are of course many other considerations. A moat has to be assessed holistically.
  12. If you can't understand a company right away, giving yourself another two months probably won't help either (a company that doesn't excite you immediately means you either don't actually understand it yet, or it just isn't your kind of company). Understanding a company quickly requires enough background knowledge — that's the so-called circle of competence. One good way to understand a company is to reason backward: ask the leadership of companies in that industry which competitor they would make disappear if they could.
  13. Cola has no taste memory.
  14. As long as it's a good business, timing doesn't matter that much. Buffett said his biggest mistakes were the times he understood an industry but didn't act on it, missing out on a lot of opportunities.
  15. Don't dwell on the past.
  16. The reason he says he doesn't study macro is that macro questions can't be predicted, but a good business you can understand is knowable.
  17. Quiet thinking is the best way to invest. Too noisy an environment gets in the way of thinking.
  18. Buy a company and hold it for the long term, without setting a target price. (Buffett only buys companies he's willing to hold for a lifetime.)
  19. If you really understand a business, you shouldn't hold more than six names (Buffett said his own top positions are more comfortably kept to about 3). Otherwise, just go extremely diversified (the S&P 500). Many of the successful investors he knows don't diversify at all.

My reflections

  1. Do the right thing. For example, back in May and June this year, my heavy allocation to leveraged ETFs plus buying stocks in companies I didn't understand were both clearly things I shouldn't have done. Once you do things you shouldn't, given enough time, you will absolutely pay a heavy price for it.
  2. The most important considerations in investing: is it a good business, how is management, is the moat deep and durable enough, is there a second or even third growth driver ahead, is it certain enough, is the price right — and of course, most important of all, you have to actually understand it yourself first.
  3. Do what you love — the happiness from it shouldn't be measured against money.
  4. If a good opportunity comes along, you have to be brave enough to bet on it. Time the market takes priority over timing in the market.
  5. Don't do meaningless things, including predicting macro timing, dwelling on the past, and so on.
  6. Long-term, quiet thinking is the foundation of investing.

Where I disagree

I basically don't disagree with any of this — I'm working hard to move in this direction myself. The one place where I hold a reservation is that I still factor in timing to some degree. After all, I don't have Berkshire Hathaway's insurance float, and my tolerance for unrealized losses is definitely lower than Buffett's. This has nothing to do with investment judgment — it's more a question of how deep your "war chest" is.

One thing I can actually use

  • ☐The core of it all: do the right thing, and don't do what you shouldn't.
