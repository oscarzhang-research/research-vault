Thesis in one sentence

The market is severely underestimating Micron Technology's intrinsic value. The reasons: HBM's inherent wafer penalty combined with HBM price recovery; almost no new entrants in HBM (the barriers to entry are extremely high); AI-driven demand for DRAM and NAND that keeps rising while no meaningful new supply comes online before 2028 (i.e., a supply–demand imbalance that exceeds anything seen in past cycles); SCAs that raise the floor on Micron's profitability; and Micron's product mix improving, with the share of data-center-related revenue rising sharply. Putting all of this together, I think Micron's target price should be $1,400 — 12x Micron's FY2026 Q4 net income, rolled forward one year.

Core logic

  1. HBM inherently carries a wafer penalty: producing the same number of bits in HBM consumes roughly four times as many wafers as traditional DRAM. I won't go deep into the technical reasons (advanced packaging, through-silicon via (TSV), thermal management, chemical materials, stacking process, and so on). HBM is a direct cause of this storage shortage — arguably the root cause, since HBM is what AI data centers are shortest on. In other words, the most direct source of incremental structural demand is the increase in HBM demand. Samsung, Micron, and SK hynix have tilted more resources toward HBM, which has driven wafer consumption far above historical levels. Combined with AI data centers' insatiable appetite for HBM, this has produced the current storage supercycle, which is now spreading from HBM to DRAM and then to NAND and HDDs. My own caveat: the structural problem on the supply side will eventually get solved. Structural changes on the supply side can only extend the cycle temporarily — they can't eliminate it. Truly extending or eliminating the cycle requires structural change on the demand side, and that demand-side structural change is indeed happening.
  2. No new entrant will show up in HBM for years to come, including CXMT. The technical barriers are simply too high, so Samsung, Micron, and SK hynix firmly control the split of this pie. That means only three players can fix the root cause of the storage supply shortage — HBM. In theory, as long as these three maintain some degree of supply discipline, or as long as technological progress doesn't resolve HBM's wafer-penalty problem too quickly, this AI storage supercycle could persist until the three players bring HBM's wafer penalty down to a level that restores supply–demand balance. My own caveat: the supply side can't be relied on to stay disciplined. High profits will inevitably drive substantial improvement in HBM yields — or at least some improvement — and that improvement will likely ease the supply–demand tightness and push spot prices down. That said, the prevailing market view right now is that HBM's yield ramp remains a persistent bottleneck, since HBM4E and HBM5 will only get harder.
  3. Demand for HBM is enormous and possibly bigger than the market imagines. The current consensus among several investment banks is that the HBM market will exceed $100B in 2027 and reach $150–200B in 2028. If that consensus holds, and assuming Samsung, Micron, and SK hynix keep their current HBM market shares, Micron would generate $20B in HBM revenue in 2027 and $30–40B in 2028 (a more conservative estimate puts Micron at $15B in 2027 and $20–25B in 2028; I lean toward the optimistic scenario). Also, according to the latest authoritative data, HBM's current per-bit margin is actually lower than traditional DRAM's — which is abnormal in a market economy, where the harder-to-make product should command a higher price when everything is in short supply. The market expects HBM's per-bit price to roughly double by 2027 (this is already baked into the TAM figures above). As HBM margins rise, Samsung, Micron, and SK hynix will only keep tilting more resources toward HBM (see the $950B in "letter-of-intent" orders Samsung and SK hynix signed with U.S. tech giants in July). Tilting more capacity toward HBM will simply keep pushing up traditional DRAM and NAND prices. My own caveat: the rate of change in DRAM and NAND price increases is expected to slow sharply by 2026 Q3, because consumer electronics pricing is already close to its limit — further price hikes would trigger demand destruction in consumer electronics. That said, this should keep revenue and net income growing (note: Micron is expected to maintain roughly 20% supply growth through 2026–2027). [In its latest earnings report, Micron said it has already booked $1B in HBM4 revenue, and that HBM4 will reach mature production much faster than HBM3E did — meaning its HBM4 revenue ramp should be quite steep.]
  4. AI's ever-rising demand for DRAM and NAND, combined with too little supply growth, is stretching out this cycle. SK hynix's leadership believes this storage supercycle will last at least through 2030 — that's their view, not mine. Right now the supply–demand gap is indeed still widening, and 2027 is expected to be the worst year (market consensus). Even by 2028, the supply–demand relationship is only expected to ease slightly from a state of total shortage — and this is actually one of the biggest points of disagreement in the market right now. Looking at Micron's own financials, CMBU plus CDBU (i.e., data center) combined now account for 60% of revenue. CMBU grew 78% QoQ and CDBU grew 103% QoQ — the two fastest-growing of Micron's four business units — and both are likely to keep growing at a fast clip. (Enterprise SSD revenue alone has already reached $5B for the quarter and is growing at a rate that doubles; Micron also disclosed that NAND prices rose over 80% that quarter, outpacing DRAM's 60%-plus increase.) The most worrying segment is MCBU, because consumer electronics pricing is already close to its ceiling — but MCBU only accounts for 28% of revenue and that share is likely to keep declining. The pleasant surprise is AEBU (automotive and robotics): management says L2-level assisted-driving vehicles use five times as much storage as ordinary cars, that L2-level penetration will double this year to 20%, and double again to 40% by 2030, and that a single robot needs ten times as much storage as an L2 vehicle. AEBU currently accounts for only 11% of Micron's total revenue. If management's assumptions hold, this segment could grow from $4.6B per quarter today to $12B per quarter three years out (allowing for possible delays in robot volume ramp and possible declines in storage prices) — a staggering number. On my own rough estimate, three years from now AEBU revenue could match or even fully surpass consumer-electronics revenue.
  5. SCAs raise the floor on Micron's profitability. Micron has signed 16 SCAs so far; 14 of them together carry a cumulative minimum revenue commitment of $100B, backed by $22B in customer cash deposits, covering 20% of DRAM and 33% of NAND. They set a floor price and a ceiling price — the ceiling is pegged to the 2026 Q2 spot price, while the floor is set above the peak of any previous cycle (I'll assume for now that the floor price implies a gross margin of roughly 70–75%). It's worth thinking about this alongside SanDisk's New Business Model (NBM), since the take-or-pay logic is the same. Based on a rough calculation — assuming Micron's current 85% gross margin, no step-change in capex, and a weighted average contract duration of 4 years — the default rate on these SCAs should be very low. Customers would only have an incentive to default if spot prices fell 40% below the floor, and would only have an incentive to renegotiate if spot fell more than 50% (assuming these contracts run through 2028). Under current conditions, a 50%-plus decline in spot prices looks unlikely, but if it happened, it would genuinely hurt Micron's revenue and net income. [Working backward from SanDisk management's comments: the mainstream market forecast is that AI-related NAND demand will reach 400EB in 2026, about 30% of total NAND demand, and 610EB in 2027, about 40% of total NAND demand. SanDisk management believes global enterprise SSD demand will reach 1.2ZB by 2030, which lines up with the mainstream market forecast. On this trajectory, total storage demand from 2026 to 2030 would roughly triple, while SK hynix management has explicitly said its own expansion plan targets 2030 capacity at 2x today's level — meaning the supply shortage could genuinely persist past 2028.] Micron management's goal is to eventually bring at least half of its bit shipments or revenue under the SCA framework — this is standard practice across storage companies today — and I think this is very credible and achievable (SanDisk, for reference, targets 50% NBM coverage by 2027 and 67% by 2028). I think it's likely Micron's management hits a similar target by 2028 (something to keep tracking). If we assume Micron locks in SCA coverage of 50% of DRAM and 67% of NAND by 2028, its SCA-guaranteed revenue would run roughly $250–350B (accounting for continued spot price appreciation, especially HBM repricing), with a weighted average duration of 4 years (the current industry standard). That implies a minimum annual revenue floor of roughly $75B going forward — even if spot prices fell 50%, Micron would still book roughly $70B in revenue, since our assumption is that customers only have a real incentive to default once spot falls more than 40% below the floor. Based on current data, Micron's FY2026 Q4 revenue is projected at $50B, and FY2027 revenue is likely to exceed $300B [achievable with 15% higher shipment volume and 30% higher spot prices], of which roughly $150B would come from spot sales. Even in the market's most pessimistic 2028 scenario — the year when risk is expected to concentrate — Micron would likely still generate around $150B in revenue, while maintaining roughly 75% gross margin and roughly 60% net margin (i.e., $90B in net income). And as storage demand keeps rising into 2029, combined with higher shipment volumes (holding prices flat versus 2028), Micron should be able to earn at least $100B. As long as AI structural demand holds up, $100B in net income is close to the floor of what this company can earn — or at least close to it. If Micron can sustain $100B in net income, its valuation multiple is very likely to get rerated. At 15x 2028 net income, Micron's valuation should be around $1.5 trillion, implying a share price of $1,300. Micron management has also said that starting later this year it will return excess cash to shareholders; factoring in buybacks would push the target price even higher (Micron's free cash flow this past quarter was $18B; based on the 2027 revenue estimate, Micron's 2027 free cash flow could reach a staggering $150B, and cumulative free cash flow over the next two years could exceed $200B). Accounting for WACC, though, the share-price uplift from buybacks is largely offset — so I'm keeping the target price at $1,300. [Note: all of this assumes a 50% spot price decline in 2028. Any scenario more optimistic than that pushes the target price above $1,300 — I plan to build a model to test this going forward.]

Key assumptions and tracking signals

  • ☐Whether HBM capacity keeps getting booked out well in advance (as of now, capacity through 2027 is already fully allocated, even for DRAM)
  • ☐Whether DRAM and NAND contract prices start falling — or rising less than expected — earlier than the market anticipates
  • ☐Whether HBM yields improve substantially (wafer penalty declining ahead of schedule)
  • ☐Whether Micron rigorously executes management's plan to raise SCA coverage and customer guarantees
  • ☐Whether CXMT's capacity expansion causes consumer-grade DRAM to saturate early, ending the price rally sooner than expected
  • ☐Whether new fab output timelines get pulled forward (Samsung, Micron, SK hynix)
  • ☐Whether HBM pricing gets repriced upward
  • ☐Whether the top-five hyperscalers' capex growth holds up (this is the upstream variable driving HBM demand)

What would prove me wrong

What would make me admit I'm wrong:

  1. NAND and DRAM contract prices hit an inflection point earlier than the market expects (this also counts if price increases simply fall short of expectations sooner than expected)
  2. HBM contract prices fail to rise meaningfully, and per-bit margins remain below traditional DRAM
  3. CXMT's capacity expansion far outpaces market expectations (the biggest price gains right now are coming from traditional DRAM, so if CXMT's rapid expansion causes traditional DRAM prices to collapse early, Micron's revenue and net income would take a real hit)
  4. Micron fails to follow through on its stated strategy to raise SCA coverage and customer guarantees, and fails to keep growing its revenue share outside consumer electronics
  5. New fab output comes online earlier than expected
  6. The top-five hyperscalers' capex growth falls short of expectations

Where I differ from consensus

What the market believes: Micron is a traditional cyclical stock.

Why I differ: HBM's customization heavily dilutes its cyclicality. Combined with HBM's unavoidable physical wafer penalty and AI's enormous demand, this cycle could sustain a long-term supply–demand imbalance. That imbalance is what's driving long-term agreements (LTAs), which raise the floor on Micron's profitability. At the same time, entirely new demand from humanoid robots and autonomous driving should bring in substantial incremental revenue, hedging against the risk of consumer electronics pricing topping out.
