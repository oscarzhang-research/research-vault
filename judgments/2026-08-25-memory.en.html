Thoughts on the Memory Industry and Latest Developments

Thesis in one sentence

The memory cycle is nowhere near its peak. Leading memory companies should see further valuation re-rating ahead. This note focuses on what makes AI-driven structural demand different from past cycles, and what's structurally different about the memory industry itself this time.

Core logic and latest industry developments

1. I've written in previous notes, many times, about why this memory cycle might really be different. The single biggest reason I keep coming back to is AI-driven structural demand. In this note I want to lay that out in full — what exactly AI-driven structural demand is, and why it's different. Let me start with some background on the traditional cyclical industry. I've described the traditional memory cycle transmission mechanism before: shortage emerges → profits surge → capex restarts → an 18-to-30-month lag → a flood of bit supply → prices collapse → production cuts plus capex cuts → supply bottoms → back to the start. We're currently in that 18-to-30-month lag phase. What could break the chain that normally follows? Either new capacity comes online and demand still outstrips it, or supply and demand settle into a new equilibrium — one where spot or contract prices can't crash all at once the way they used to. Right now the market is basically pricing leading memory companies out to 2027 — the consensus view is that earnings peak in 2027 and then decline sharply. That's evident in forward P/E multiples of 4–7x, which is exactly how you'd price a traditional cyclical stock. Look at past cycles — 1993–1996, 2002–2005, 2007–2009, 2016–2019, 2020–2023. Every single time, spot prices got cut in half, then cut in half again. During memory downturns, memory makers weren't just short of net profit — they were nearly always losing money outright. The root cause of that lack of profit in every past downturn was oversupply: it drove spot prices down so hard that selling memory actually became a negative-gross-margin business. It all comes down to oversupply. And the root cause of oversupply is a mismatch between supply and demand timing. DRAM supply is highly inflexible — from breaking ground on a fab, through yield ramp, to full-volume delivery takes 3–5 years. Demand spikes can happen far faster than that. By the time a fab is built and running at scale, demand may no longer need that much supply. Digging deeper: why does demand fail to sustain itself, and why does supply overshoot? On the demand side, every past demand surge — the PC era, the iPhone era, the 2021 pandemic era — was ultimately capped by one dominant factor: the number of people. The demand function was always bounded by headcount times devices-per-person. On the supply side, because DRAM and NAND are standardized commodity products, once a fab is built it can ship at full volume immediately. Combined with the huge upfront capex that forces memory makers to run fabs at full utilization, and the total lack of product differentiation, the moment supply catches up, prices crash — there's no long-term agreement (LTA) protecting the price. Beyond building new fabs, supply can also grow through process node improvements — i.e., density gains — which further accelerates bit output and worsens the oversupply.

2. Why do I think 'this time is different'? Let's break it down using the framework above. Start with demand. Every prior cycle was ultimately shackled to 'number of people.' The training and inference demand of the AI era is fundamentally not dependent on headcount — or at least it's no longer bound to that single root cause. Put simply: memory no longer primarily serves a fixed number of 'human endpoints.' It has become a key input to generating intelligence itself. Daily AI inference calls are growing exponentially and are nowhere near saturation. Every enterprise, every consumer app, every autonomous vehicle, every AI agent is an incremental consumer of memory bandwidth. Here's a more intuitive way to see how the demand side has changed. The old memory-demand formula was: number of people × devices per person × memory per device. The first two terms are bounded; the third grows linearly. The new memory-demand formula is: number of sessions × context length × KV cache per token × number of parallel sub-tasks. Three of these four terms have no clear upper bound — and none of the old demand has gone away. Number of sessions still depends somewhat on headcount, but it's clearly loosening that constraint — a person will almost certainly own only one phone, but the number of queries that same person asks can keep rising as AI matures, and that growth may well not be exponential, but it's growth beyond headcount nonetheless. Context length has gone from 8K to 128K to 1M tokens. Agents need to fit an entire codebase, an entire document, an entire conversation history into context — a single 1M-token session alone requires 310GB. As primary tasks get harder, the number of parallel sub-tasks will keep rising. More parallel sub-tasks means previously prepared KV caches get evicted, forcing a fresh prefill pass and consuming even more cache — and some sub-tasks run for extremely long durations. (Per NVIDIA's own public data, average output tokens per query have grown more than 5x per year since the second half of 2024.) None of these three drivers grows on the premise of 'number of people' — and rising headcount actually makes the caching problem worse. In effect, the importance of headcount as a factor has collapsed: it's gone from being the load-bearing variable to just icing on the cake. This incremental demand is entirely new — it's the single biggest structural difference from the past. Put more simply: the ceiling on demand has shifted from 'number of people' to 'number of tokens,' and the token count is really a function of enterprises' willingness and ability to pay. That ability to pay is obviously a much higher ceiling — the top-five hyperscalers in the US are all raising capex, and have committed to even higher capex through 2027–2028. But that ability to pay ultimately depends on whether the hyperscalers and companies buying the memory actually make money. Whether the hyperscalers (the midstream) make money depends on whether AI model companies (OpenAI, Anthropic) or downstream application companies make money. The time it takes for a negative shock to transmit back up this chain is far longer than in a traditional cycle — I mean in the downside case, where downstream companies fail to turn a profit for a long time. Everyone already knows AI is the future trend, and it is genuinely raising productivity and generating real economic value (look at the blowout earnings from downstream application companies like PLTR). The only open question is whether that value can cover the cost. Even while it can't yet cover the cost, hyperscalers will keep raising money and using every other means to keep betting on AI for the future — because stepping back is the same as admitting defeat. There's also a higher level of national competition at play here, which means state machinery will run at full throttle to keep this industrial revolution going for as long as possible.

3. Now let's turn to why the supply side is different too. To be clear again: supply-side changes don't really break the cycle, they only extend it temporarily — if profits are high enough, supply will always find a way to ramp up, that's an economic law you can't get around. First, memory now has a genuinely customized product — HBM4 and every generation of HBM after it. It comes with a wafer penalty, advanced packaging bottlenecks, and base-die customization. Right now the advanced packaging bottleneck is arguably worse than the HBM manufacturing itself, because HBM can never route around advanced packaging, and advanced packaging capacity is almost entirely locked up at TSMC, whose current capacity is limited. HBM's own stacking and yield issues are also far more severe than standardized DRAM ever was. Second, DRAM density improvement is approaching its physical limit, so the era of growing bit output without building new fabs is basically over — this is a physical bottleneck, worth looking into on your own. Consider that in the future Vera Rubin racks, LPDDR5X accounts for 61% of the Vera Rubin NVL200 rack's 'memory and storage budget.' That DRAM can no longer add bits through process improvements — the only way left is to add wafer capacity by building new fabs. The single most important change on the supply side is the emergence of HBM, which has turned memory from a fully standardized commodity into a customized product. That's clearly a constraint on supply — but commercially, it's an unambiguous positive for the memory industry.

4. Worth mentioning long-term agreements (LTAs) again. In the past, the moment oversupply hit, memory makers' profits would collapse or even turn negative, because all their memory was exposed to spot prices. LTAs now lock a meaningful share of volume in at contract prices, and because of deposits and guarantees, the odds of a customer breaching an LTA are low — the mainstream deposit ratio is currently 25% of the LTA value, and it will only go higher from the second half of 2026 through 2027. On top of that, memory makers' gross margins are simply too high right now (while capex as a share of revenue keeps declining), so even in an extreme scenario where spot prices get cut in half, they can still hold onto a level of profitability that never existed before. At an 85% gross margin, even a 50% spot price cut still leaves a 70% gross margin — higher than any historical peak — and that's before accounting for the extensive LTA protection memory makers now have.

5. From demand to supply to the price mechanism, every link in the chain that used to cut memory cycles in half has changed substantially — in some cases fundamentally. That's why I'm very optimistic about the memory industry's future. That doesn't mean there's no risk, though: a slowdown in the rate of change of contract prices, a collective wave of new capacity coming online in 2028, CXMT's capacity expansion, and inventory ticking up from its trough are all somewhat concerning signals. Ultimately, it all comes down to whether demand really is 'unlimited' — whether hyperscalers and enterprises keep doubling down on memory investment, and whether downstream AI companies can eventually cover their capex with real profit. My own caveat: supply-side factors can't be the core thesis for this supercycle, because every supply problem eventually gets solved.

6. Samsung has recently doubled down on ZHBM, while SK Hynix is considering an even more cutting-edge advanced packaging approach (IHBM). Both are fundamentally aimed at solving the 'memory wall' problem and improving AI inference efficiency. Whether it's ZHBM or IHBM, advanced packaging remains one of the bottlenecks — which may make it an attractive investment opportunity going forward.

7. NVIDIA announced it's cutting back SOCAMM allocation in Vera Rubin racks, driven by continued DRAM supply tightness through 2027 plus uncertainty around HBM4e qualification progress and yield. Beyond NVIDIA, some CSPs are also considering reducing HBM capacity on their next-generation in-house ASICs. It all comes down to supply falling short of demand.

What would prove me wrong

  1. If the cache window lengthens or the premium falls, that signals DRAM constraints easing; the reverse signals them tightening.
  2. NVIDIA's data shows output tokens growing over 5x per year since the second half of 2024. If that curve flattens, KV cache demand growth flattens with it.
  3. The 2026 target is 100–120K wafers/month, versus roughly 2.7M wafers of 2027 demand (1.22M from NVIDIA alone). How fast this gap closes determines when the bottleneck rotates elsewhere.
  4. 6F² can only carry through to the 1d node. 1c is already in mass production; 1d is expected within the next 1–2 years. If 1d is still 6F², the stall in density improvement will drag on longer.
  5. In December 2018, Micron's CEO was saying demand would recover even while the company was already cutting NAND capex — actions spoke the truth before the words did.
  6. Inventory rebounds quickly.

What this means for my positioning

I'm sticking with a heavy overweight in memory. I strongly believe memory is due for a valuation paradigm shift.
