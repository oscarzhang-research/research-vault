Thoughts on the Memory Industry and Latest Developments

Thesis in one sentence

The memory cycle is nowhere near its peak, and I expect leading memory companies to see further valuation re-rating ahead. This note focuses on how the structural demand created by AI differs from past cycles, and on what's different about the memory industry itself this time.

Core logic and latest industry developments

  1. I've written many times before about why this memory cycle might really be different. The biggest reason, in my summary, is the structural demand driven by AI. In this note I want to spell out exactly what that means and why it's different — starting with some background on the traditional cyclical pattern.

    I've described the transmission mechanism of the traditional memory cycle before: shortage emerges — profits surge — capex restarts — an 18–30 month lag — a flood of bit supply — prices collapse — production cuts plus capex cuts — supply bottoms out — back to the start. We're currently in that 18–30 month lag phase. What could break the usual transmission from here? Either new capacity comes online and demand still outstrips it, or supply and demand settle into a new equilibrium that prevents spot or contract prices from falling too sharply in one shot.

    Right now the market's pricing of the leading memory companies is essentially concentrated on 2027 — the consensus across the market is that memory-company earnings will peak in 2027 and decline sharply after that (which is why these companies currently trade at 4–7x forward P/E, priced the way a traditional cyclical stock would be).

    Look at the past cycles — 1993–1996, 2002–2005, 2007–2009, 2016–2019, 2020–2023. Every single time, spot prices got cut in half, then in half again. Memory makers were essentially loss-making (not just lacking net profit) during every one of these downturns. The root cause: oversupply crushed spot prices to the point where selling memory became a negative-gross-margin business — fundamentally, it was oversupply.

    The underlying cause of that oversupply is a mismatch between supply and demand. DRAM supply is highly inflexible — it takes 3–5 years from breaking ground to yield ramp to full-capacity delivery — while demand can surge in a much shorter window. By the time a fab is built and ramped to mass production, demand may no longer need that much supply.

    Digging deeper: why does demand fail to sustain itself, and why does supply overshoot? On the demand side, every past demand surge was ultimately capped by "the number of people" — whether in the PC era, the iPhone era, or the 2021 pandemic era (the demand function was always bounded by population size times devices per person). On the supply side, DRAM and NAND are standardized products, so once a fab is built, it can ship at scale almost immediately (and given how much capital was already sunk, memory makers are compelled to run at full utilization). With no product differentiation, the moment supply catches up, prices crash — there's no long-term agreement (LTA) to cushion the fall. Beyond building new fabs, suppliers can also boost bit output through process improvements (i.e., density gains), which further accelerates oversupply.

  2. Why do I think "this time is different"? Let me unpack that using the framework above, starting with demand.

    Past cycles could never escape the gravitational pull of "the number of people." AI-era training and inference demand, by contrast, is not fundamentally tied to headcount — or at least it's no longer dependent on that single root variable. Put simply: memory is no longer primarily serving a fixed number of "human endpoints" — it's becoming a critical input to generating intelligence itself. The number of daily AI inference calls is growing exponentially and is nowhere near saturation. Every enterprise, every consumer app, every autonomous vehicle, every AI agent is an incremental consumer of memory bandwidth.

    Here's a more concrete way to frame the shift in demand. The traditional memory-demand formula was: number of people × devices per person × memory per device — the first two terms are bounded, and only the third grows linearly. The new demand formula is: number of sessions × context length × KV cache per token × number of parallel sub-tasks — three of these four terms have no clear upper bound (and none of the old demand has gone away).

    Number of sessions is still loosely tied to population, but it's clearly breaking free of that constraint to some degree — a person will likely only ever own one phone, but the number of queries a person asks may keep rising as AI matures, and that growth path probably isn't going to be exponential. Context length has gone from 8K to 128K to 1M — agents need to load entire codebases, entire documents, entire conversation histories into context (a single 1M-token session alone requires 310GB). Parallel sub-tasks will only multiply as the difficulty of the primary task grows, and more parallel sub-tasks mean the KV cache that was already prepared gets discarded, forcing a full re-prefill that consumes even more cache (some sub-tasks run for extremely long durations). According to Nvidia's own disclosures, the average number of output tokens per query has been growing at over 5x per year since the second half of 2024.

    None of these three drivers grows on the basis of "number of people" — and in fact, population growth makes the cache problem worse (which effectively demotes the importance of headcount from a make-or-break variable to a nice-to-have). This increment is entirely new — it's the single biggest structural difference from the past.

    To put it even more simply: the ceiling on demand has shifted from "number of people" to "number of tokens" — and the number of tokens is really a function of enterprise willingness and ability to pay. That's obviously a much higher ceiling (all of the top-five hyperscalers in the U.S. are currently raising capex, and some have even committed to raising capex further in 2027–2028).

    Whether that willingness to pay holds depends on whether the hyperscalers and companies spending on memory can actually make money — and that, in turn, depends on whether AI model companies (like OpenAI or Anthropic) or downstream application companies can turn a profit. The time it takes for a negative outcome to feed back through this chain — i.e., for the downstream layer to fail to become profitable — is far longer than in past cycles, because everyone already knows AI is the future trend, and it is genuinely raising productivity and generating real economic value (look at the blowout earnings from downstream application companies like PLTR). The real question is whether that value can eventually cover the cost. As long as it can't yet, hyperscalers will keep financing and betting on AI for the future by whatever means necessary — because backing out is an admission of defeat. And layered on top of all this is a higher-stakes national rivalry, which means state machinery will run at full throttle to keep this industrial revolution going for as long as possible.

  3. Now for why the supply side is also different (to be clear again: supply-side changes will never truly break the cycle — they can only extend it temporarily, because as long as profits are high enough, suppliers will always find a way to ramp their own output — that's an economic law that can't be escaped).

    First, memory now has a genuinely customized product — HBM4 and every generation of HBM after it — which carries a wafer penalty, an advanced-packaging bottleneck, and base-die customization. The advanced-packaging bottleneck is currently even more severe than HBM manufacturing itself, because HBM can never bypass advanced packaging, and advanced-packaging capacity is almost entirely locked up at TSMC, whose current capacity is finite. HBM's own stacking and yield issues are also far more severe than those of standardized DRAM.

    Second, DRAM density gains are approaching their physical limit, so the era of boosting bit output without building new fabs is essentially over — this is a physical bottleneck, not a temporary one. In the future Vera Rubin rack, for example, LPDDR5X will account for 61% of the "memory and storage budget" of a Vera Rubin200 rack, and that DRAM can no longer gain more bits through process improvements — the only way to add supply is to build more fabs and process more wafers.

    The single most important supply-side change, though, is the emergence of HBM, which is shifting memory from a fully standardized product to a customized one. That's clearly an obstacle on the supply side — but commercially, it's an unambiguous positive for the memory industry.

  4. One more point on long-term agreements (LTAs). In the past, the moment oversupply hit, memory makers' profits would crash immediately — even turn negative — because all of their memory was exposed to spot prices. LTAs now lock in a substantial share of supply at contract prices, and thanks to deposits and guarantees, the odds of customers breaking an LTA are very low (the mainstream guarantee ratio is currently 25% of the LTA volume, and it's only going to go higher in the second half of 2026 into 2027).

    On top of that, gross margins at memory makers are currently extremely high (while capex as a share of revenue keeps declining), so even in an extreme scenario where spot prices get cut in half, memory makers can still maintain profitability that would have been unheard of in past cycles. At an 85% gross margin, even if spot prices are cut in half, the company would still be left with a 70% gross margin — higher than any historical peak — and that's before accounting for the protection offered by the large volume of LTAs memory makers now hold.

  5. From demand, to supply, to the pricing mechanism — every one of the traditional links that used to send memory prices crashing has changed substantially, in some cases fundamentally. That's why I'm very bullish on the future of the memory industry. That said, this doesn't mean there's no risk: a declining rate of change in contract prices, the collective release of 2028 capacity, CXMT's capacity expansion, and inventory levels ticking up from their lows — all of these are somewhat dangerous signals worth watching.

    Ultimately, everything still comes down to whether demand really is "unbounded" — that is, whether hyperscalers and enterprises keep doubling down on memory investment, and whether downstream AI companies can genuinely generate enough profit to cover their capex. My own caveat: supply-side dynamics can't be the core reason this becomes a supercycle, because every supply problem eventually gets solved.

  6. Samsung has recently doubled down on its bet on ZHBM, while SK Hynix is considering an even more cutting-edge advanced-packaging approach (IHBM). Both are fundamentally aimed at solving the "memory wall" problem and improving AI inference efficiency. Whether it's ZHBM or IHBM, advanced packaging remains one of the bottlenecks — and this might turn out to be one of the better investment opportunities going forward.

  7. Nvidia has announced a reduced SOCAMM configuration in the Vera Rubin rack, driven by continued tight DRAM supply into 2027 plus uncertainty around HBM4e validation progress and yields. Beyond Nvidia, some CSPs are also considering trimming the HBM capacity on their next-generation in-house ASICs. At bottom, this is simply a case of demand outstripping supply.

Key data and tracking signals

  • ☐Whether cache-write window pricing across model APIs is increasing.
  • ☐Whether the growth rate of average output tokens per query is holding up.
  • ☐Whether TSMC's actual CoWoS monthly capacity is ramping quickly.
  • ☐The mass-production timing of the 1d node, and whether it uses 4F².
  • ☐Whether capex allocation is shifting direction.
  • ☐Whether customer-side (rather than supplier-side) inventory remains tight.

What would prove me wrong

  1. A longer cache-write window or a lower cache premium = DRAM constraints easing; the reverse = constraints tightening.
  2. Nvidia's own data shows output tokens per query growing at over 5x per year since the second half of 2024. If that curve flattens, growth in KV cache demand will flatten in lockstep.
  3. The 2026 target is 100–120K wafers per month, versus roughly 2.7 million wafers of demand in 2027 (Nvidia alone accounts for 1.22 million). How fast that gap closes determines when the bottleneck rotates.
  4. 6F² can only carry the industry through the 1d node; 1c is already in mass production, and 1d is expected in the next 1–2 years. If 1d is still 6F², the stall in density improvement will persist longer.
  5. In December 2018, Micron's CEO was saying demand would recover even as the company was already cutting NAND capex — actions revealed the truth before the words did.
  6. Inventory rebuilding quickly.

What this means for my positioning

I'm sticking with a heavy overweight in memory. I firmly believe memory is heading into a re-rating of its valuation paradigm.
