Thesis in one sentence

SanDisk's Investor Day unambiguously confirmed many of the core points from my previous note, and it gave investors much greater confidence in the durability of profitability in particular. I think this NAND upcycle will exceed any prior cycle, especially in how long profitability holds up. That said, the Investor Day also exposed some unknown risks, so this isn't a case for blindly going long.

Core logic

  1. At Investor Day, SanDisk laid out a three-year financial model for FY2028–FY2030: gross margin target of 80%, operating margin of 75%, free cash flow margin of 50%, and revenue growth sustained in the mid-to-high teens. Every single one of these numbers is well above any prior cycle, and the model covers FY2028–2030 — precisely the three years the market is most skeptical about when it comes to storage-cycle durability. If this model holds, SanDisk's valuation gets completely rewritten, because it means that in the years the market most doubts the durability of storage profitability, SanDisk would still be running a high-margin, high-free-cash-flow business. Based on SanDisk's own FY28–FY30 free cash flow model, EPS for FY28/FY29/FY30 works out to roughly 215/250/280. Applying a 10x P/E to FY28 gives a share price around 2150. Current mainstream sell-side estimates put SanDisk's FY27 EPS at around 235, meaning this model already bakes in a downturn for SanDisk in FY28 (though that's not necessarily much of a margin of safety). Using the simple DCF model I built earlier and applying a 10x P/E to FY27 net income — a multiple upgrade justified by the improved visibility on earnings durability that came out of Investor Day — SanDisk's share price would come to around 2350. (Note: SanDisk plans to return 100% of excess cash to shareholders this time; I'll separately work out how much float such buybacks would retire. The current buyback plan is...)
  2. SanDisk's long-term financial model rests on two pillars: New Business Model (NBM)'s protection against price downside, and the persistently high margins driven by AI's ever-growing demand for NAND (a prolonged supply–demand imbalance) plus SanDisk's own technology progress. Let's start with why supply and demand could stay imbalanced for a long time — I covered most of this in my previous note, so here I'll go deeper. On the demand side, SanDisk management believes the NAND market will grow from $300bn to $500bn between CY26 and CY27, with supply staying tight through 2028, and that the enterprise flash TAM will reach 1.2ZB by 2030 (essentially treat this as unlimited, given AI data centers' insatiable appetite for KV cache). Let's reverse-engineer this using SanDisk's own numbers to see if it's plausible. SanDisk currently accounts for roughly 12% of the NAND market. Its Q2 2026 revenue was $8.9bn, and next-quarter guidance midpoint is $10.5bn — roughly in line with (if anything, a bit conservative relative to) the implied 2027 NAND market size. By SanDisk's own account, two-thirds of last quarter's revenue growth came from price increases and one-third from higher shipment volumes. If NAND prices need to rise another ~40% to get the market from $300bn to $500bn (i.e., another 40% increase after SanDisk's next earnings release, which I think would actually be difficult) — and that 40% is on top of, not including, the 10–15% increase TrendForce forecast for 2026Q3 — I still think that's a stretch. Price increases in NAND for consumer electronics are close to topping out (a plateau, at minimum, is consensus), and enterprise SSDs for AI data centers are about the only category still seeing price hikes, though those should keep rising. Assuming NAND prices rise 20% in 2027, plus volume growth (assuming it holds at roughly 20%, consistent with the latest earnings), the 2027 NAND market would be around $440bn. At SanDisk's 12% share, that implies revenue of roughly $52bn. Even on a conservative 11% share, SanDisk's 2027 revenue would still be about $48.4bn. Of course, if the weighted-average price increase falls short of 20%, SanDisk's revenue would take a hit (and a meaningful share of SanDisk's bit shipments are already locked into NBM contracts — nobody knows how much of any spot price upside those locked-in volumes would actually capture). Now to the supply side. Before 2028, the consensus view is that essentially no capacity expansion capable of resolving the supply–demand imbalance will come online (the market should be in deficit almost 100% of the time through 2028). Risk gets concentrated in 2028, when SK hynix's Yongin Y2 plus Cheongju M17, Samsung's Pyeongtaek P5, Micron's Hiroshima plus Idaho expansions, and YMTC's expansion (a matter of national policy) all come into play. Samsung's Pyeongtaek P5 can produce HBM/DRAM/NAND, but the read is that any incremental NAND capacity there is likely just capacity converted away from DRAM rather than a net add. SK hynix's Yongin Y2 plus Cheongju M17 effective supply will most likely be pushed out to 2029; Yongin Y1 has 150k wafers/month of capacity, but it's prioritized for HBM/DRAM. Micron's Singapore Fab10b won't start producing wafers until the second half of 2028, and its other fabs are focused on DRAM and HBM (Micron's latest earnings call said Micron's NAND supply growth in FY2026 will lag industry-wide supply growth). Bottom line: because HBM carries higher margin per wafer, Samsung/SK hynix/Micron's main focus remains DRAM and HBM, leaving limited NAND capacity additions (YMTC is the bigger swing factor here). Continued rapid demand growth against limited supply additions, combined with HBM's inherent wafer penalty (loss per bit is roughly four times that of standard DRAM), means this supply–demand imbalance could last longer than any prior cycle. That doesn't mean there's no risk. Whether NAND supply and demand stays balanced essentially comes down to whether Samsung/SK hynix/Micron are willing to add NAND capacity — not being willing now doesn't mean they won't be willing later, especially with NAND margins now this attractive. Even though AI-driven structural demand growth is enormous, any excess returns from a supply–demand imbalance can still be ended by a capacity expansion, particularly given that SanDisk isn't a top-tier NAND producer. My own caveat: in SanDisk's long-term model, gross margin holds at roughly 80% and all the revenue growth comes from bit-shipment growth — so whether the 2028 spot price can stay at a relatively high level is critical. If it doesn't, that risks triggering contract defaults, which I address in the next point.
  3. NBM's protection for SanDisk's earnings floor is real — I covered the specific numbers in my previous note. But there's a detail I hadn't fully appreciated before: if 2028 spot prices fall far enough below the NBM floor price, major customers could plausibly default. So the $93.9bn minimum-revenue floor implied by the ten NBM agreements isn't actually SanDisk's true floor — the real floor is the $16.5bn in guaranteed payments (this portion is mandatory; defaulting on it triggers immediate settlement). Assume remaining obligations at the start of FY28 are roughly $60bn (total contract value of $93.9bn minus a rough estimate of what's already been fulfilled). If spot price falls 30% below the floor price, defaulting starts to become the economically favorable choice (though the gap is small at that point, and I don't think these mega-customers would default over that little money). If spot price falls 50% below the floor price, the probability of default (or contract renegotiation) rises substantially. If we assume spot prices fall that far in 2028, the effective floor on that $93.9bn in minimum revenue is really only around $80bn. Even at $80bn, I still don't think SanDisk's current valuation is expensive. (Though if this scenario plays out, SanDisk's current price would no longer be attractive — under the company's plan, a third of 2028 volumes won't be covered by NBM, and if that uncovered spot exposure gets cut in half in price, it would cut SanDisk's revenue by roughly 16%.) Remember, SanDisk's current NBM coverage doesn't even reach half of bit shipments — if SanDisk keeps signing more NBM agreements with customers going forward, it'll pick up more guaranteed payments (especially given that the 2027 storage shortfall looks even larger than 2026's, which could push the guarantee ratio even higher). NBM genuinely gives SanDisk more clarity on sustaining long-term profitability, but the real downside protection comes from the guaranteed-payment amount, not the minimum-revenue figure in the contracts.
  4. SanDisk's own technology progress plus a favorable business model is driving exceptionally high margins. The CBA (CMOS directly Bonded to Array) technology unveiled at Investor Day manufactures the memory array wafer and the peripheral CMOS control-circuit wafer separately, then bonds them together, rather than building both on the same wafer. NAND's cost-reduction cadence has historically been dictated almost entirely by node transitions, and a node transition costs several billion dollars and takes two to three years. If SanDisk can genuinely insert high-performance derivative products between node generations using an 'old array + new CMOS' approach, it gains a kind of product agility that peers don't have — in an environment where AI customer requirements shift every six months, that's a real competitive advantage, and it can substantially cut capex while lifting margins. At the same time, SanDisk's fab cost-sharing arrangement with Kioxia further controls capex, and its operating costs are extremely low (barely growing over the past several quarters). At current margin levels, even if SanDisk's bit-shipment price were cut in half, gross margin would still be 70%! Of course, as shipment volumes rise going forward (per SanDisk's own long-term financial model), capex will inevitably increase, including fab depreciation. But higher output also means unit costs keep falling, so my read is that SanDisk's operating costs will stay low — meaning gross margin can stay elevated for a long stretch, relative to past cycles.

Key assumptions and tracking signals

  • ☐Whether NAND contract prices continue rising in Q3 (essentially, whether the supply–demand gap keeps tightening)
  • ☐Whether YMTC's capacity expansion comes earlier than expected (or is larger than expected)
  • ☐Whether consumer SSD/DIMM spot prices show a quarter-over-quarter decline
  • ☐Whether hyperscalers cut capex
  • ☐Whether SanDisk raises NBM coverage and enterprise SSD mix as planned (to hedge against YMTC's expansion and sustain or even improve margins)

What would prove me wrong

Here's what would make me admit I was wrong:

  1. Contract prices show zero growth or decline in any quarter
  2. YMTC's enterprise SSDs pass qualification with a major hyperscaler customer, or YMTC's rapid capacity expansion forces consumer electronics price increases to end early or even reverse into declines
  3. Non-GAAP gross margin falls more than 3 percentage points quarter-over-quarter, or quarterly operating costs rise above $1.8bn
  4. The top-four hyperscalers' 2027 capex guidance comes in below $900bn combined
  5. Consumer SSD/DIMM spot prices show a quarter-over-quarter decline

Where I differ from consensus

What the market thinks: SanDisk is a highly cyclical company.

Why I differ: NBM, a persistent supply–demand imbalance, SanDisk's own product-mix improvements, and the High Bandwidth Flash (HBF) outlook could together substantially dampen its cyclicality.
